The Abandoned Shelf

Breaking circle-packing records that nobody had touched since 2011
2026-08-07 · overnight solver campaign · every claim certified in 40-digit arithmeticcomputational geometry

In 2026, AI systems swept the famous packing tables. AlphaEvolve, GPT-5.6, SAT solvers and the amateurs wielding them reset records on Erich Friedman's Packing Center, pushed the no-three-in-line problem, closed Kobon triangle cases. If a geometry problem had a public leaderboard, someone pointed a modern search at it this year.

Almost. Packomania — the reference site for packing equal circles into containers, maintained by Eckard Specht — has pages the rush never reached. The semicircle, the circular quadrant, and the isosceles right triangle hold 1,149 best-known packings between them, and the newest of them dates to July 2011. They were computed by a handful of people — Specht himself, Claudia López Soto, David A. Paterson, John E. Beasley — on the hardware of fifteen years ago, and then everyone moved on to more fashionable containers.

So two nights ago I pointed a solver at the shelf. This is what fell off.

The problem, and how to attack it

The task is always the same: place n equal circles inside a fixed container so that the common radius r is as large as possible. No circle may cross the boundary, no two circles may overlap. Every record on those pages is a claim of the form "here are coordinates achieving radius r; nobody knows better ones."

The attack is unfashionable too — no language models, no SAT encodings, just a careful version of the thing that set the records in the first place:

Polish. Formulate max-r as a nonlinear program (maximize r subject to boundary clearances and pairwise separations) and run SLSQP with analytic gradients, starting from the published record coordinates themselves. For efficiency, only near-touching pairs enter the constraint set, regenerated until the active set stabilizes.

Shake. Perturb the record configuration with Gaussian noise at several amplitudes and re-polish. This is basin-hopping around the incumbent: most shakes fall back into the same local optimum, but sometimes the configuration crosses a ridge and lands somewhere better.

Restart. Fresh random configurations, polished from scratch — cheap lottery tickets against the possibility that the record's whole neighborhood is wrong.

None of this is clever. What makes it work in 2026 is that a £300 desktop runs roughly a hundred polishes per instance where a 2010 workstation budgeted far fewer, and n between 50 and 150 is exactly the regime where the energy landscape has too many local optima for anyone's 2010 compute to have explored properly.

Proof, not trust

Solver output is not evidence — SLSQP reports "optimal" with constraint violations at whatever tolerance it was given. Every claim here passes through an unconditional certificate instead. For fixed centers, the largest feasible radius is simply the minimum over all boundary clearances and all half pairwise distances — a closed-form expression, no optimization involved. I evaluate it from explicit coordinates in 40-digit arithmetic (mpmath) — not formal interval arithmetic, but with rounding error near 10⁻³⁸ against claimed gains no smaller than 10⁻¹¹, a wrong verdict would need the arithmetic to be off by twenty-seven orders of magnitude. If that certified value exceeds the published 30-digit record, the record is beaten, regardless of how the coordinates were found. A Levenberg–Marquardt pass on the tangency system of each packing's rigid core, run at 50-digit precision, then turns the raw solver output into submission-grade coordinates.

What it means

Two honest readings, and both are true.

The deflationary one: these gains are microscopic. The largest improves the radius in the seventh decimal place; the smallest, the eleventh. No circle got meaningfully bigger. If you drew the old and new packings on paper they would be indistinguishable — only the certified arithmetic can tell them apart.

The other one: a best-known value in a mathematical table is a statement about the entire history of attempts against it, and dozens of such statements from the Specht era just failed simultaneously — not to a new idea, but to the same algorithms with fifteen years more compute behind them. The pattern in the scatter says something specific: The frontier between "surely optimal" and "merely unchallenged" in these tables sits exactly where 2010-era compute ran out of patience. Below it, the records look like mathematical truths; above it, they were snapshots of hardware.

And the rearrangements are real: in the showcased instances, beating the record required moving circles by up to a third of a radius and rewiring dozens of tangencies — these are different packings, not sharpened decimals of the old ones.

Method: SLSQP polish / shake / restart from published coordinates; unconditional feasibility certificates and Levenberg–Marquardt tangency polish in mpmath (40–50 digits).
